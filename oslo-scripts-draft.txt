STOP 1 — ROYAL PALACE
Tag: Stop 1 of 12 - Slottsparken
Tagline: The king who never moved in
WalkNext: 568m, about 7 min to next stop
Photo: oslo1.jpg (landscape)
Audio: oslostop1.mp3
Tip: Changing of the Guard — The changing of the guard takes place daily at 13:30. The palace interior is open for guided tours during a period in summer. Check the official royal palace website for current dates and how to book.

SCRIPT:
The palace at the top of Karl Johans gate was completed in 1849. Norway had been in union with Sweden since 1814, and the palace was built for Karl Johan, the Swedish-Norwegian king who gave his name to the main street below you. He never lived in it: Karl Johan died in 1844, five years before the building was finished. His son Oscar I was the first king to actually inhabit it.|||The architect was Hans Linstow, a Dane who had settled in Norway. The brief called for something that looked royal without looking grandiose. Linstow designed a main wing and two lower flanking wings around a semicircular entrance courtyard. The result is dignified and relatively modest by the standards of European royal palaces. The grounds, Slottsparken, were laid out at the same time and are open to the public year-round.|||The statue of Karl Johan on horseback in front of the palace was unveiled in 1875. The king faces down toward the city, as if looking out over the kingdom he built. Karl Johan was born Jean-Baptiste Bernadotte, a French general under Napoleon, and was adopted by the childless Swedish king Charles XIII as his heir in 1810. He founded the Bernadotte dynasty that still sits on the throne today. Norway's current king, Harald V, was born in 1937 and has lived in the palace since his accession in 1991.|||Head southeast down Karl Johans gate. The National Theatre is about 568 metres ahead, seven minutes at an easy pace.

Tip: The Two Statues — Look closely at the two statues in front of the theatre: Ibsen on the left, Bjornson on the right. They face different directions. They were rivals in life and contemporaries knew it. The positioning was not accidental.

SCRIPT:
The National Theatre was built between 1891 and 1899. The architect was Henrik Bull. Outside the main entrance stand two statues: Henrik Ibsen to the left and Bjornstjerne Bjornson to the right. Both face away from each other and out toward the street. They were rivals in life, and the positioning was deliberate.|||Ibsen is the reason this building matters. He spent much of his creative life in exile in Germany and Italy, writing plays that were considered scandalous when they appeared. A Doll's House, Hedda Gabler, Ghosts, The Wild Duck. These were the plays that changed what theatre could say about marriage, inheritance, deception, and the lives people are trapped into by society. When Ibsen returned to Norway permanently in 1891, after twenty-seven years abroad, it was to a city that had finally caught up with him. The National Theatre was built in part to provide a stage worthy of his work.|||The theatre opened on 1 September 1899 with a performance of Ibsen's Kongsemnerne, known in English as The Pretenders. Ibsen attended. He was seventy-one. He would die seven years later, in 1906, in an apartment a short walk from here.|||Bjornson, the other figure in stone, was also a playwright and novelist but is remembered today primarily as the lyricist of the Norwegian national anthem. He won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1903.|||Continue east along Karl Johans gate. The Grand Hotel is about 371 metres ahead, five minutes.

The Grand Hotel opened at this address in 1874. He sat at the same table, at the same time each afternoon. Edvard Munch painted a version of the interior: the regulars, Ibsen's table, the particular afternoon light of the room.|||The Parliament building, Stortinget, is approximately 104 metres away. STOP 4 — STORTINGET
Tag: Stop 4 of 12 - City Centre
Tagline: A Swedish architect builds Norway's parliament
WalkNext: 428m, about 5 min to next stop
Photo: oslo4.jpg (landscape)
Audio: oslostop4.mp3
Tip: Guided Tours — The debating chamber and parts of the building are open for free guided tours on selected days. Check the Stortinget website for the current schedule. Entry is free but you need to book in advance.

SCRIPT:
The Parliament building was completed in 1866. The architect was Emil Victor Langlet, a Swede who won the competition. The building was controversial from the start: a Swedish architect winning the contract to design Norway's parliament, in a country that had been asserting independence from Sweden since 1814.|||Norway's constitution was signed at Eidsvoll on 17 May 1814. The date is still the national day. The constitution created a parliament, the Storting, and declared Norway a sovereign state. In the same year, Norway entered a union with Sweden that would last until 1905. The parliament building was constructed half a century into that union. Having a parliament at all was an assertion of Norwegian identity within a union where Sweden held most of the external political power.|||Norway has a unicameral parliament: one chamber with 169 representatives elected by proportional representation. The Storting sits for four-year terms. The prime minister governs by majority confidence but the parliament cannot be dissolved by the government. On the steps out front you hear street performers in summer and demonstrations in other seasons.|||Head southwest toward the waterfront. The City Hall, Oslo Radhuset, is about 428 metres ahead, five minutes.

Tip: The Main Hall — The main hall interior is open to visitors most days and entry is free. The murals by Alf Rolfsen and other Norwegian artists cover the walls from floor to ceiling. The Nobel ceremony is held in this room every 10 December.

SCRIPT:
Oslo City Hall was opened on 15 May 1950, in time for the city's 900th anniversary. The project had been planned since the 1930s, begun in 1931, interrupted by the German occupation, and completed after the war. The building took nineteen years to finish. The architects were Arnstein Arneberg and Magnus Poulsson.|||The Nobel Peace Prize ceremony is held in the main hall of this building every 10 December, the anniversary of Alfred Nobel's death. This has been the venue since 1990, when the ceremony moved from the University's Aula. The choice was deliberate: a civic building, not an academic or royal one. The committee considered it a more appropriate setting for a prize given in the name of peace and popular welfare.|||The interior of the main hall is covered in murals commissioned over decades from Norwegian artists. The dominant work is by Alf Rolfsen. The paintings depict Norwegian working life, national history, and figures from mythology. When they were unveiled, the critical reception ranged from admiration to outright hostility. Time has been kinder to them.|||The building faces the fjord deliberately, which means its least interesting side faces the city. Norwegians tend to refer to it in affectionately unflattering terms. The Nobel Peace Center is about 243 metres southwest, three minutes on foot.

STOP 8 — AKERSHUS FORTRESS
Tag: Stop 8 of 12 - Akershus
Tagline: Seven centuries on the same promontory
WalkNext: 450m, about 6 min to next stop
Photo: oslo8.jpg (landscape)
Audio: oslostop8.mp3
Tip: Resistance Museum — The Norwegian Resistance Museum inside the fortress is one of the most compelling museums in Oslo. It documents the occupation from 1940 to 1945 in specific, human terms. Entry is not free: check current admission prices on the official website. The views from the fortress ramparts across the fjord are also worth taking in before you leave.

SCRIPT:
Akershus Fortress was built by King Haakon V of Norway, beginning around 1299. The location was chosen because the promontory above the fjord was the most defensible point at the mouth of the Alna river, which had been the site of the original medieval town. The fortress was intended to replace Oslo's earlier castle, which had become inadequate.|||The castle was besieged several times over the following centuries and was substantially expanded and modernised by King Christian IV in the early seventeenth century. Christian IV built Renaissance palace sections within the medieval walls that are still visible today. He also rebuilt Oslo itself in 1624 after the city burned, renaming it Christiania after himself.|||During the Second World War, Akershus was used by German occupying forces as a prison and execution site. Members of the Norwegian resistance were imprisoned here and some were shot within the walls. The fortress contains the Norwegian Resistance Museum, which documents the occupation of Norway from 1940 to 1945. It is one of the most visited museums in Oslo and one of the most specific in terms of what it describes: this is where it happened.|||Head north and east from the fortress into the old town. Christiania Torv is about 450 metres northeast, six minutes.

SCRIPT:
The Oslo Opera House was designed by the Norwegian architecture firm Snohetta and opened in April 2008. The building sits directly at the waterline of the Oslofjord, and its most distinctive feature is the sloping white marble roof that descends from the ridge of the building to the water, forming a surface that pedestrians can walk on year-round.|||The marble came from Carrara in Italy. Approximately 36,000 individually cut pieces cover the roof and the exterior walls of the main building. The slope is gentle enough to walk up comfortably and steep enough to create a dramatic angle when viewed from the water. From the top of the roof you can see across the fjord to the islands and, on clear days, to the hills on the far shore.|||The building won the European Union Prize for Contemporary Architecture, the Mies van der Rohe Award, in 2009. The jury described it as a building that had become a meeting point for the citizens of Oslo without being designed specifically for that purpose. The roof is not officially an observation deck, but it functions as one.|||The interior holds the main auditorium with 1,364 seats. The opera company is the Norwegian National Opera and Ballet. Tours of the interior are available on selected days: check the official website for current times and prices.|||The Munch Museum is about 255 metres south, three minutes on foot, following the waterfront promenade.
