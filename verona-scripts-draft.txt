STOP 1 — Castelvecchio
walkNext: "500m, about 6 min to the Arena"

You're standing at the western edge of the old city, where the Adige river curves before bending back around Verona's ancient core. In front of you is Castelvecchio, the great fortified castle of the Scaligeri dynasty, built in the fourteenth century when this family ruled Verona with a grip of iron, and considerable style.|||The castle was built around 1354 by Cangrande II della Scala, one of the more ruthless members of the dynasty. He wanted a fortress that could defend against external enemies and, crucially, provide an escape route if his own citizens turned on him. The bridge to your side, Ponte Scaligero, served exactly that purpose: a private passage to the far bank of the river, where a garrison could be kept loyal and a horse kept saddled.|||Look at the bridge. Three wide Gothic arches spanning the Adige, with crenellated battlements running along the top. It was destroyed in the Second World War and rebuilt in the 1950s using salvaged original stone. The castle now houses one of Verona's finest art collections, arranged inside a building that was redesigned in the 1960s by architect Carlo Scarpa, one of the most admired museum renovations in Italy.|||Head east along the main road about six minutes toward Piazza Brà and the Arena.

STOP 2 — Arena di Verona
walkNext: "200m, about 3 min to Porta Borsari"

Piazza Brà opens up around you, and there it is: the Arena, nearly two thousand years old and still holding its shape with extraordinary confidence. It sits at the southern edge of the old city, wide and low compared to the Colosseum in Rome, but arguably more complete. This is one of the largest Roman amphitheatres ever built, and it is still in use.|||Construction began in the first century AD. The Arena was built outside the city walls at the time, since amphitheatres were loud, violent places, and the Romans kept them at a respectful distance from civic life. Verona eventually grew around it, and the Arena became the centre of gravity it still is today. At its peak it held an audience of around thirty thousand people, who came to watch gladiatorial combat, animal hunts, and public executions.|||What you see is most of the original structure, plus a fragment of the outer ring on the northwest corner, the so-called Ala, the wing, which survived while the rest of the outer wall was lost, probably in a medieval earthquake. The Arena is still used every summer for the Verona Opera Festival, one of the most celebrated outdoor opera events in the world. The scale of the building transforms performance: the stage sets are enormous, the acoustics surprisingly good, and the audience numbers in the thousands. On opening night, every seat holder is given a candle, and at a signal the entire amphitheatre lights up. Fifteen thousand flames in a two-thousand-year-old Roman arena.|||Walk north about three minutes to Porta Borsari.

tipLabel: "Opera Festival"
tipText: "The Verona Opera Festival runs in the Arena each summer, typically June to September. Tickets sell fast. Check the official Arena di Verona website for dates, programmes, and booking."

FACT-CHECK FLAGS: First century AD construction, ~30,000 capacity, Ala on northwest corner, medieval earthquake claim, candle tradition on opening night, ~15,000 candles figure.

STOP 3 — Porta Borsari
walkNext: "620m, about 8 min to Casa di Giulietta"

Porta Borsari is easy to walk past, but stop here for a moment because what you're looking at is nearly two thousand years old. This is one of the main gates of Roman Verona, the point where the Via Postumia, the great consular road running across northern Italy, entered the city. Every merchant, soldier, and dignitary who arrived in Verona came through here.|||The gate dates from the first century AD, though there was certainly an earlier structure on the same spot. Look at the facade: two arched passageways flanked by columns and decorative windows above. Carved across the top, a Latin inscription survives that dates the gate and names the emperor under whom it was built: the stone itself is the evidence. It has almost the feel of a theatre frontage rather than a defensive gate. That is deliberate. Roman city gates were designed to impress as much as to control. This one says: you are entering somewhere significant.|||The name Porta Borsari comes from medieval toll collectors, the bursarii, who stood here and charged passing traders for the right to enter the city. The Roman name was Porta Iovia. The gate has been built into and around so many times over the centuries that it now sits embedded in the street, with later buildings attached on either side. Step back if you can and take in the full height of it.|||Head east about eight minutes through the old city to Casa di Giulietta.

tipLabel: "Worth knowing"
tipText: "The gate is free to view from the street at any time."

STOP 4 — Casa di Giulietta
walkNext: "100m, about 1 min to Piazza delle Erbe"

You've probably found it by the crowd. Casa di Giulietta draws more visitors than almost any other site in Verona. Step into the courtyard and look up at the balcony. It is, frankly, a modest one. But none of that matters, because what this place is selling is not architecture. It is a story.|||Shakespeare set Romeo and Juliet in Verona, probably because Italian cities had a reputation for passionate drama and his audiences found it convincing. He almost certainly never visited. The families of Montague and Capulet are loosely based on real Veronese clans: the Montecchi and the Cappelletti. This building belonged to the Cappello family, which gave it its connection. The balcony was added in the 1930s specifically to give visitors somewhere to look.|||The walls of the entrance passage are covered in padlocks and written messages. The bronze statue of Juliet in the courtyard was installed in 1972. Her right breast has been rubbed shiny by millions of hands following a tradition supposed to bring luck in love. Whether it works is not something Stepcast can verify.|||Head north a minute to Piazza delle Erbe.

STOP 5 — Piazza delle Erbe
walkNext: "90m, about 1 min to Piazza dei Signori"

Piazza delle Erbe is the oldest public space in Verona. It sits directly on the footprint of the ancient Roman forum and has been a gathering place, a market, and the civic heart of this city for more than two thousand years. The long, narrow shape of the square follows the Roman original exactly.|||Look around. At the north end, the Torre dei Lamberti rises 84 metres above the roofline, a medieval tower built by a wealthy merchant family and added to over several centuries. In the centre, the fountain holds a Roman statue known as Madonna Verona, dating from the first century AD, though she has stood here in this form since the fourteenth century. The column topped with the winged lion of Saint Mark is a reminder that Verona spent over three centuries under Venetian rule, from 1405 until Napoleon arrived in 1797.|||The square is still a market today, though it sells tourist goods and fresh produce rather than the grain and livestock it once traded. The buildings around you mix Gothic, Baroque, and Renaissance in the effortless way Italian cities do, each century adding its layer without too much concern for consistency.|||Step through the arch at the eastern end of the piazza to reach Piazza dei Signori, just a minute away.

tipLabel: "Torre dei Lamberti"
tipText: "The tower has a lift and offers views across the whole city and surrounding hills. Check the official website for opening times and current prices."

FACT-CHECK FLAGS: Roman forum footprint claim, Torre dei Lamberti 84m height, Madonna Verona statue first century AD, fourteenth century placement in current form, Venetian rule 1405-1797, which end of piazza the arch is on.

STOP 6 — Piazza dei Signori
walkNext: "77m, about 1 min to Arche Scaligere"

Step through the arch and you move from the noise and colour of Piazza delle Erbe into something more formal. Piazza dei Signori, also called Piazza Dante, is where Verona conducted its official business. These buildings were the machinery of power.|||The statue in the centre is Dante Alighieri, placed here in 1865. He gazes toward the Palazzo degli Scaligeri, acknowledging the family that gave Dante shelter during his years of exile from Florence. Cangrande I della Scala, the greatest of the Scaligeri lords, was one of Dante's most important patrons, and Dante mentioned him in the Divine Comedy with considerable warmth: unusual praise from a poet not known for generosity toward the powerful.|||The Loggia del Consiglio on the north side, the arcaded building with the painted facade, is a late fifteenth century Renaissance structure, considered one of the finest of its kind in the Veneto. The palazzo with the Gothic windows dates from the Scaligeri period. And beneath the square, during excavations in recent decades, significant Roman remains were found. Another layer of the city that never quite disappears.|||Head northeast a minute to the Arche Scaligere, just around the corner.

FACT-CHECK FLAGS: Dante statue placed 1865, which direction Dante gazes (verify against actual statue orientation), Cangrande I as Dante's patron confirmed in Paradiso Canto XVII, Loggia del Consiglio on north side and late 15th century, Roman remains found beneath square.

STOP 7 — Arche Scaligere
walkNext: "300m, about 4 min to Sant'Anastasia"

The Arche Scaligere are the tombs of the Scaligeri, the family that ruled Verona for over a century and whose fingerprints are on almost everything you have seen today. These are not modest memorials.|||The monuments rise above the iron fence like Gothic rockets: tall, pinnacled tabernacles with equestrian statues on top, each lord depicted at full gallop in full armour, riding into eternity with the same energy he presumably rode through life. The highest figure is Cangrande I della Scala, who ruled from 1308 to 1329 and presided over Verona's greatest period of expansion. The face you see here is a copy. The original equestrian statue, famous for its wide grin, is the one in the Castelvecchio museum you passed at the start of the tour.|||The Scaligeri named themselves with a certain swagger: Cangrande means Big Dog, Mastino means Mastiff, Cansignorio means Lord Dog. They ruled Verona for over a century, and were, by the standards of medieval northern Italy, capable and relatively stable. One more thing: Scala means ladder in Italian. Look at the ironwork fence surrounding the tombs right now and you will see the ladder repeated as an emblem. The family who styled themselves the Dog Lords chose a ladder as their symbol. Their dynasty ended when Gian Galeazzo Visconti of Milan took the city in 1387, and the lords who had commissioned these extraordinary tombs were gone within a generation.|||Head northeast about four minutes to the Basilica di Sant'Anastasia.

FACT-CHECK FLAGS: Cangrande I ruled 1308-1329, name translations (Big Dog, Mastiff, Lord Dog), Scala=ladder confirmed, ladder on fence, Gian Galeazzo Visconti took city 1387, original Cangrande statue in Castelvecchio museum.

STOP 8 — Basilica di Sant'Anastasia
walkNext: "300m, about 4 min to Ponte Pietra"

Sant'Anastasia is the largest church in Verona, and it announces itself with an almost deliberate roughness. The Dominicans began building it in 1290 and never completed the facade, which has been left in bare brick for seven centuries. Whatever was planned for the front never arrived. The result, oddly, suits the building.|||Inside, the space is large and light, with slender columns running the length of the nave. Near the entrance, two stone figures crouch under the weight of holy water stoups. They are known as the Gobbi, the hunchbacks, and have stood here for over five centuries, each one slightly different, the subject of various local stories about who they represent.|||In the Pellegrini chapel, on the right as you enter, is a fresco by Pisanello painted around 1436, showing Saint George and the Princess of Trebizond. It is one of the great paintings of the International Gothic, detailed and dreamlike, hovering at the exact point between medieval and Renaissance. The horses in it are extraordinary: precise, muscular, full of life. It alone makes the church worth entering.|||Walk north and slightly east about four minutes to Ponte Pietra.

STOP 9 — Ponte Pietra
walkNext: "270m, about 3 min to Teatro Romano"

Ponte Pietra is the oldest bridge in Verona, with origins going back to the first century BC. It crosses the Adige at its most photogenic point, and from the middle of the bridge you get one of the best views in the city: the curve of the river, the hill rising ahead of you, and the rooftops of the old city behind.|||The bridge has five arches, but they are not all the same age. The two on the far side are the Roman originals. The rest were rebuilt during the medieval period. In 1945, the retreating German army destroyed the bridge, as they did with most of the Adige crossings. After the war, Veronese citizens recovered the original stone blocks from the riverbed and the bridge was rebuilt between 1957 and 1959 using as much original material as possible.|||Look up at the hill ahead. The battlements of Castel San Pietro are visible near the top, where this tour ends. Below the castle, cut into the hillside, you can make out the curved seating of Teatro Romano, where you are heading next.|||Cross the bridge and walk north a short distance to Teatro Romano at the foot of the hill.

FACT-CHECK FLAGS: Oldest bridge in Verona claim, first century BC origins, five arches total, which arches are Roman originals, German army destroyed in 1945, citizens recovered stones from riverbed, rebuilding 1957-1959.

STOP 10 — Teatro Romano
walkNext: "400m, about 8 min to Castel San Pietro — steep climb"

Teatro Romano is one of those Roman structures that surprises by its completeness. Built into the hillside in the first century BC, it used the slope of San Pietro hill as natural support for its banks of seating, a technique the Romans used wherever terrain allowed. What you see is original Roman stone, in a setting it has occupied for over two thousand years.|||The theatre held around three thousand spectators, which gives a sense of the difference in scale between theatre and arena in Roman Verona. The Arena was for mass spectacle. The theatre was for more refined entertainment: comedy, tragedy, mime. The same city that watched gladiators in the afternoon came here in the evening for Plautus and Terence.|||The building above the theatre, reached by a lift from the site, is a former convent now housing the Archaeological Museum, one of the better small Roman collections in northern Italy. The theatre itself hosts a summer festival of theatre and dance that has been running since 1948.|||The steps to Castel San Pietro begin at the far end of the site. They are steep and take about eight minutes. You are heading for the viewpoint at the top.

STOP 11 — Castel San Pietro
walkNext: "Final stop"

You made it. Stop here, catch your breath, and look.|||The view from this terrace is the one that appears on every postcard of Verona. The Adige loops below you, the terracotta rooftops fill the valley, and if the light is right you can trace almost the entire route you have just walked. Castelvecchio and the Ponte Scaligero are the tower and bridge to the west. The ellipse of the Arena sits in the middle distance at the south end of the old city. Ponte Pietra and the curve of Teatro Romano's seating are directly below you.|||The building behind you is a former Austro-Hungarian barracks, built in the 1850s on the foundations of earlier fortifications. The Visconti built a castle here after taking Verona from the Scaligeri in 1387, and the Venetians reinforced it when they arrived in 1405. By the time the Austrians came, the structure had been through centuries of use, rebuilding, and neglect. Their barracks replaced it. The building is not remarkable in itself. The point of this hill has always been the view.|||Verona has been a Roman colony, a seat of emperors, a medieval signoria, a Venetian province, and an Austro-Hungarian garrison town. It has been conquered, besieged, flooded, and bombed, and each time it has repaired itself and carried on. The route you have just walked is, in a very direct sense, the same route its citizens have been walking for two thousand years. You just did it with better audio.
